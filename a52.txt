    <h1>Reading the Fight</h1>
    <p class="lede">The same two rules — hit, don't get hit — but four very different ways of going about it. This issue looks at stance, style, footwork, defence, and the corner team working behind the scenes.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="hero-photo">
    <img src="https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1526792903869-f3035bfc7f29?fm=jpg&q=80&w=1200&auto=format&fit=crop" alt="A boxer resting against the ring ropes between rounds">
    <div class="photo-credit">Wade Austin Ellis / Unsplash</div>
  </div>

<main>
  <div class="wrap intro">
    <p>Two boxers can follow the exact same rulebook and still fight completely different sports. One plants their feet and looks to end things early; another spends twelve rounds refusing to be hit at all. Neither is wrong — style in boxing is less a matter of taste and more a set of trade-offs between range, risk, and stamina.</p>
    <p>Where our first issue covered the ring, the rules, and the equipment, this one covers the craft: how a fighter stands, how they move, how they defend, and who is working in their corner between rounds. As before, this is a reference, not a pitch for anything.</p>
  </div>

  <!-- ROUND 01 -->
  <section class="round wrap" id="stance">
    <div class="round-head">
      <span class="round-num">ROUND&nbsp;01</span>
      <h2>Stance: Orthodox &amp; Southpaw</h2>
    </div>
    <div class="round-body">
      <p>Every boxer fights from one of two mirror-image stances, chosen mostly by which hand is naturally stronger and more accurate.</p>
      <h3>Orthodox</h3>
      <p>The left foot and left hand lead, with the stronger right hand held back near the chin, ready to cross. The large majority of right-handed boxers fight orthodox, since it lets their dominant hand deliver the power shot from the rear.</p>
      <h3>Southpaw</h3>
      <p>The mirror image: right foot and right hand lead, left hand held back for power. Many southpaws are naturally left-handed, though some orthodox fighters train to switch as a tactical option mid-fight.</p>
      <p>An orthodox fighter meeting a southpaw creates an "open" stance matchup, where both lead feet sit on the same side rather than opposite — this changes the angles for the jab and the rear hand and is widely considered awkward for fighters who rarely train against it.</p>
    </div>
  </section>

  <figure class="band">
    <img src="https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1606335543042-57c525922933?fm=jpg&q=80&w=1600&auto=format&fit=crop" alt="A boxer in fighting stance wearing black boxing gloves">
    <figcaption class="wrap">A tight, high guard — hands up, chin down, weight balanced between both feet.</figcaption>
  </figure>

  <!-- ROUND 02 -->
  <section class="round wrap" id="styles">
    <div class="round-head">
      <span class="round-num">ROUND&nbsp;02</span>
      <h2>Four Fighting Styles</h2>
    </div>
    <div class="round-body">
      <p>Trainers and commentators generally sort boxers into four broad archetypes. Few fighters fit one category perfectly — most blend two — but the labels are a useful shorthand for how someone is likely to approach a fight.</p>
      <div class="style-grid">
        <div class="style-card">
          <h4>Out-boxer</h4>
          <p>Fights at long range, relies on the jab, footwork, and reach to score points while avoiding exchanges. Wins on volume and control rather than one-punch power.</p>
        </div>
        <div class="style-card">
          <h4>Swarmer / pressure fighter</h4>
          <p>Closes distance constantly, works behind a tight guard, and throws high volumes of combinations to the body and head to wear an opponent down.</p>
        </div>
        <div class="style-card">
          <h4>Slugger / puncher</h4>
          <p>Trades power for speed and volume, looking to end a fight in a single exchange rather than win a long, technical contest.</p>
        </div>
        <div class="style-card">
          <h4>Counter-puncher</h4>
          <p>Fights off the back foot deliberately, drawing an opponent's shots and answering with precise, well-timed counters rather than initiating.</p>
        </div>
      </div>
      <p>Match-ups between styles are part of what makes boxing hard to predict: a pure out-boxer against a swarmer is a very different fight than an out-boxer against a counter-puncher, even if both opponents are similarly skilled.</p>
    </div>
  </section>

  <div class="duo">
    <img src="https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1636581563884-39569e81cbad?fm=jpg&q=80&w=1000&auto=format&fit=crop" alt="A boxer wearing a white and black boxing glove, extending a punch">

  </div>

  <!-- ROUND 03 -->
  <section class="round wrap" id="footwork">
    <div class="round-head">
      <span class="round-num">ROUND&nbsp;03</span>
      <h2>Footwork &amp; Distance</h2>
    </div>
    <div class="round-body">
      <p>Punches only land within a fighter's own reach, which makes footwork — not hand speed — the skill most trainers say separates good boxers from great ones. Distance is managed in small, controlled steps rather than long strides, so balance is never lost mid-exchange.</p>

      <h3>Small steps, not strides</h3>
      <p>Moving the lead foot first when stepping forward, and the rear foot first when stepping back, keeps a fighter's base stable — crossing the feet or taking one big step leaves them off balance and open to a counter.</p>
      <h3>Cutting off the ring</h3>
      <p>Pressure fighters use angled steps rather than a straight chase to close the distance on an opponent who is circling away, limiting how much ring the retreating fighter has left to work with.</p>
    </div>
  </section>

  <!-- ROUND 04 -->
  <section class="round wrap" id="defence">
    <div class="round-head">
      <span class="round-num">ROUND&nbsp;04</span>
      <h2>Defensive Technique</h2>
    </div>
    <div class="round-body">
      <p>Boxing's defensive toolkit is built to avoid a punch entirely rather than simply absorb it — every method below keeps a fighter's hands free to counter immediately.</p>
      <h3>Guard</h3>
      <p>Hands held high near the temples, elbows tucked to protect the ribs — the default position a fighter returns to between every action.</p>
      <h3>Slip</h3>
      <p>A small lateral head movement that lets a straight punch pass by the ear rather than the chin, without moving the feet.</p>
      <h3>Roll (bob and weave)</h3>
      <p>Bending at the knees and waist to duck under a hook, then rising on the opposite side — used mainly against looping punches rather than straight ones.</p>
      <h3>Parry</h3>
      <p>Deflecting an incoming punch with a light touch of the glove, redirecting it just enough to miss.</p>
      <h3>Block</h3>
      <p>Absorbing a punch on the forearms or gloves rather than avoiding it, trading a little impact for certainty.</p>
      <h3>Clinch</h3>
      <p>Tying up an opponent's arms at close range to prevent follow-up punches — legal briefly, but a referee will break a prolonged clinch.</p>
    </div>
  </section>

  <figure class="band">
    <img src="https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1726867825984-0582b27728b8?fm=jpg&q=80&w=1600&auto=format&fit=crop" alt="Two boxers standing in a ring during a bout">
    <figcaption class="wrap">Defence is judged the same way offence is — a round of well-timed slips and blocks can win a card without landing a single hard shot.</figcaption>
  </figure>

  <!-- ROUND 05 -->
  <section class="round wrap" id="corner">
    <div class="round-head">
      <span class="round-num">ROUND&nbsp;05</span>
      <h2>The Corner</h2>
    </div>
    <div class="round-body">
      <p>A boxer is the only person allowed to throw a punch, but they are rarely working alone — a small team is permitted to sit at ringside and work during the sixty-second break between rounds.</p>
      <h3>Head trainer</h3>
      <p>Gives tactical instructions between rounds, adjusts the game plan based on what's working, and ultimately decides whether to stop the fight from the corner's side if their fighter is overmatched.</p>
      <h3>Cutman</h3>
      <p>A specialist who controls swelling and stops bleeding in the short break between rounds, using an ice-cold compress called an enswell and adrenaline-soaked cotton — without this work, many fights would be stopped early on cuts alone.</p>
      <h3>Assistant / cornerman</h3>
      <p>Handles the stool, water bottle, mouthguard, and Vaseline application to the face, keeping the sixty seconds running efficiently under pressure.</p>
      <p>Corner staff can also throw in the towel — physically tossing a white towel into the ring — as a way to stop the fight and protect their fighter when they judge it unsafe to continue, even if the boxer wants to carry on.</p>
    </div>
  </section>

  <!-- ROUND 06 -->
  <section class="round wrap" id="amateur-pro">
    <div class="round-head">
      <span class="round-num">ROUND&nbsp;06</span>
      <h2>Amateur vs. Professional</h2>
    </div>
    <div class="round-body">
      <p>Amateur and professional boxing share a rulebook but differ enough that a fighter often has to adapt their whole approach when they turn pro.</p>
      <table>
        <thead><tr><th>Aspect</th><th>Amateur / Olympic</th><th>Professional</th></tr></thead>
        <tbody>
          <tr><td>Round length</td><td>3 rounds of 3 minutes</td><td>4–12 rounds of 3 minutes</td></tr>
          <tr><td>Scoring emphasis</td><td>Clean, scoring punches counted closely</td><td>Broader judging of aggression, defence, ring control</td></tr>
          <tr><td>Headgear</td><td>Used in most youth and women's divisions</td><td>Never used</td></tr>
          <tr><td>Standing eight count</td><td>Common</td><td>Varies by commission, less common</td></tr>
          <tr><td>Governing body</td><td>World Boxing / national federations</td><td>WBA, WBC, IBF, WBO and others</td></tr>
        </tbody>
      </table>
      <p>Because amateur bouts are shorter and reward clean, visible punches, many amateur standouts are known for high work rate and out-boxing; the extended distance of professional rounds rewards patience, power management, and durability in ways a three-round amateur career never tests.</p>
    </div>
  </section>

  <!-- ROUND 07 -->
  <section class="round wrap" id="terms">
    <div class="round-head">
      <span class="round-num">ROUND&nbsp;07</span>
      <h2>Technique Glossary</h2>
    </div>
    <div class="round-body">
      <dl class="glossary">
        <dt>Feint</dt><dd>A false movement meant to draw a reaction from an opponent without committing to a real punch.</dd>
        <dt>Combination</dt><dd>A pre-planned sequence of two or more punches thrown in quick succession.</dd>
        <dt>Lead hand / rear hand</dt><dd>The forward hand (jab side) and the back hand (power side), named relative to a fighter's stance.</dd>
        <dt>Range</dt><dd>The distance at which a fighter's punches can land — long, mid, or close range each favour different tools.</dd>
        <dt>Ring IQ</dt><dd>A fighter's ability to read a fight in real time and adjust tactics, distinct from raw physical skill.</dd>
        <dt>Switch-hitter</dt><dd>A boxer who can fight comfortably from both orthodox and southpaw stances.</dd>
        <dt>Body shot</dt><dd>A punch aimed below the chest, used to slow an opponent's legs and breathing over the course of a fight.</dd>
        <dt>Check hook</dt><dd>A hook thrown while pivoting away, designed to catch a pursuing opponent and create space at the same time.</dd>
        <dt>Rope-a-dope</dt><dd>A defensive tactic of resting against the ropes to absorb and block punches on the arms, tiring an aggressive opponent before countering.</dd>
        <dt>Set-up punch</dt><dd>A punch thrown mainly to create an opening for the punch that follows it, rather than to score on its own.</dd>
      </dl>
    </div>
