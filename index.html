An Informational Guide

Reading the Fight

The same two rules — hit, don't get hit — but four very different ways of going about it. This issue looks at stance, style, footwork, defence, and the corner team working behind the scenes.

Wade Austin Ellis / Unsplash

Two boxers can follow the exact same rulebook and still fight completely different sports. One plants their feet and looks to end things early; another spends twelve rounds refusing to be hit at all. Neither is wrong — style in boxing is less a matter of taste and more a set of trade-offs between range, risk, and stamina.

Where our first issue covered the ring, the rules, and the equipment, this one covers the craft: how a fighter stands, how they move, how they defend, and who is working in their corner between rounds. As before, this is a reference, not a pitch for anything.

ROUND 01

Stance: Orthodox & Southpaw

Every boxer fights from one of two mirror-image stances, chosen mostly by which hand is naturally stronger and more accurate.

Orthodox

The left foot and left hand lead, with the stronger right hand held back near the chin, ready to cross. The large majority of right-handed boxers fight orthodox, since it lets their dominant hand deliver the power shot from the rear.

Southpaw

The mirror image: right foot and right hand lead, left hand held back for power. Many southpaws are naturally left-handed, though some orthodox fighters train to switch as a tactical option mid-fight.

An orthodox fighter meeting a southpaw creates an "open" stance matchup, where both lead feet sit on the same side rather than opposite — this changes the angles for the jab and the rear hand and is widely considered awkward for fighters who rarely train against it.

ROUND 02

Four Fighting Styles

Trainers and commentators generally sort boxers into four broad archetypes. Few fighters fit one category perfectly — most blend two — but the labels are a useful shorthand for how someone is likely to approach a fight.

Out-boxer

Fights at long range, relies on the jab, footwork, and reach to score points while avoiding exchanges. Wins on volume and control rather than one-punch power.

Swarmer / pressure fighter

Closes distance constantly, works behind a tight guard, and throws high volumes of combinations to the body and head to wear an opponent down.

Slugger / puncher

Trades power for speed and volume, looking to end a fight in a single exchange rather than win a long, technical contest.

Counter-puncher

Fights off the back foot deliberately, drawing an opponent's shots and answering with precise, well-timed counters rather than initiating.

Match-ups between styles are part of what makes boxing hard to predict: a pure out-boxer against a swarmer is a very different fight than an out-boxer against a counter-puncher, even if both opponents are similarly skilled.

ROUND 03

Footwork & Distance

Punches only land within a fighter's own reach, which makes footwork — not hand speed — the skill most trainers say separates good boxers from great ones. Distance is managed in small, controlled steps rather than long strides, so balance is never lost mid-exchange.

The four basic footwork adjustments used to control range and angle.

Small steps, not strides

Moving the lead foot first when stepping forward, and the rear foot first when stepping back, keeps a fighter's base stable — crossing the feet or taking one big step leaves them off balance and open to a counter.

Cutting off the ring

Pressure fighters use angled steps rather than a straight chase to close the distance on an opponent who is circling away, limiting how much ring the retreating fighter has left to work with.

ROUND 04

Defensive Technique

Boxing's defensive toolkit is built to avoid a punch entirely rather than simply absorb it — every method below keeps a fighter's hands free to counter immediately.

Guard

Hands held high near the temples, elbows tucked to protect the ribs — the default position a fighter returns to between every action.

Slip

A small lateral head movement that lets a straight punch pass by the ear rather than the chin, without moving the feet.

Roll (bob and weave)

Bending at the knees and waist to duck under a hook, then rising on the opposite side — used mainly against looping punches rather than straight ones.

Parry

Deflecting an incoming punch with a light touch of the glove, redirecting it just enough to miss.

Block

Absorbing a punch on the forearms or gloves rather than avoiding it, trading a little impact for certainty.

Clinch

Tying up an opponent's arms at close range to prevent follow-up punches — legal briefly, but a referee will break a prolonged clinch.

ROUND 05

The Corner

A boxer is the only person allowed to throw a punch, but they are rarely working alone — a small team is permitted to sit at ringside and work during the sixty-second break between rounds.

Head trainer

Gives tactical instructions between rounds, adjusts the game plan based on what's working, and ultimately decides whether to stop the fight from the corner's side if their fighter is overmatched.

Cutman

A specialist who controls swelling and stops bleeding in the short break between rounds, using an ice-cold compress called an enswell and adrenaline-soaked cotton — without this work, many fights would be stopped early on cuts alone.

Assistant / cornerman

Handles the stool, water bottle, mouthguard, and Vaseline application to the face, keeping the sixty seconds running efficiently under pressure.

Corner staff can also throw in the towel — physically tossing a white towel into the ring — as a way to stop the fight and protect their fighter when they judge it unsafe to continue, even if the boxer wants to carry on.

ROUND 06

Amateur vs. Professional

Amateur and professional boxing share a rulebook but differ enough that a fighter often has to adapt their whole approach when they turn pro.

AspectAmateur / OlympicProfessional
Round length3 rounds of 3 minutes4–12 rounds of 3 minutes
Scoring emphasisClean, scoring punches counted closelyBroader judging of aggression, defence, ring control
HeadgearUsed in most youth and women's divisionsNever used
Standing eight countCommonVaries by commission, less common
Governing bodyWorld Boxing / national federationsWBA, WBC, IBF, WBO and others

Because amateur bouts are shorter and reward clean, visible punches, many amateur standouts are known for high work rate and out-boxing; the extended distance of professional rounds rewards patience, power management, and durability in ways a three-round amateur career never tests.

ROUND 07

Technique Glossary

Feint
A false movement meant to draw a reaction from an opponent without committing to a real punch.
Combination
A pre-planned sequence of two or more punches thrown in quick succession.
Lead hand / rear hand
The forward hand (jab side) and the back hand (power side), named relative to a fighter's stance.
Range
The distance at which a fighter's punches can land — long, mid, or close range each favour different tools.
Ring IQ
A fighter's ability to read a fight in real time and adjust tactics, distinct from raw physical skill.
Switch-hitter
A boxer who can fight comfortably from both orthodox and southpaw stances.
Body shot
A punch aimed below the chest, used to slow an opponent's legs and breathing over the course of a fight.
Check hook
A hook thrown while pivoting away, designed to catch a pursuing opponent and create space at the same time.
Rope-a-dope
A defensive tactic of resting against the ropes to absorb and block punches on the arms, tiring an aggressive opponent before countering.
Set-up punch
A punch thrown mainly to create an opening for the punch that follows it, rather than to score on its own.

This page is a general reference on style and technique and does not replace instruction from a qualified coach — coaching cues vary between gyms and traditions.
